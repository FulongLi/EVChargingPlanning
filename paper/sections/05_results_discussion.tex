\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Service-Zone Partitioning and Selected Sites}
Fig.~\ref{fig:london_geo}, Fig.~\ref{fig:london_demand}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:london_grid} show the selected stations obtained from geographical K-means, demand-weighted K-means, and the proposed grid-aware demand clustering method. Each figure is generated from the same Barnet OpenStreetMap-derived demand grid, candidate-site set, and administrative-boundary outline. The difference is the feature basis used to form service zones before optimisation. In the figures, coloured dots represent demand grid points and their cluster membership, grey rings represent candidate charging sites, black circles represent selected charging stations, and the black line marks the Barnet boundary. Longitude and latitude ticks are included to make the spatial scale and location easier to interpret.

The geographical baseline in Fig.~\ref{fig:london_geo} is generated using longitude and latitude only. It therefore shows spatially compact service zones, but it does not directly account for local demand intensity or grid-risk variation. The demand-weighted result in Fig.~\ref{fig:london_demand} uses demand weights during spatial clustering, which shifts the zones toward higher-demand grid points. The grid-aware result in Fig.~\ref{fig:london_grid} uses spatial location, demand, accessibility, and grid-risk proxy features, allowing grid-risk information to influence the decomposition stage before stations are selected.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:london_results} reports the main quantitative results. Geographical K-means achieves the shortest average service distance, 1.05 km, and full demand coverage in the clipped Barnet case. The demand-weighted method provides a similar user-side result, with an average service distance of 1.08 km and a coverage ratio of 98.25\%. The direct demand-hotspot and existing-station baselines perform worse on coverage and service distance, showing that simply selecting high-demand or existing charging locations is insufficient without service-zone decomposition.

The proposed grid-aware method gives a different trade-off. Its average service distance is 1.07 km and its coverage ratio is 98.69\%, both close to the geographical and demand-weighted clustering results. At the same time, it lowers grid impact per installed charger to 4.92, compared with 6.38 for geographical K-means and 6.14 for demand-weighted K-means. This indicates that embedding grid-risk information in the partitioning stage can move the solution toward lower-risk candidate sites without creating a large user-distance penalty in the boundary-clipped Barnet case.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Cluster Number and Grid-Risk Weight}
A sensitivity analysis was conducted over the number of clusters and the grid-risk feature weight. The composite score combines normalised service distance, grid impact per charger, utilisation imbalance, unserved demand, and coverage. The best-performing configuration in this grid search is \(K=8\) with a grid-risk feature weight of 0.50. The second-best configuration is \(K=10\) with a grid-risk weight of 0.75. This suggests that moderate grid awareness is beneficial, but excessive grid-risk weighting can fragment service zones and reduce user-side performance.

\subsection{Feature Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_tradeoff} report a feature ablation study. The ablation starts with spatial-only clustering, then adds demand weighting or demand as a feature, then adds accessibility, and finally adds grid risk. The final grid-aware setting reduces grid impact per charger from 6.38 in the spatial-only case to 4.92, while maintaining a high coverage ratio. This supports the claim that the grid-risk feature changes the planning outcome rather than merely relabelling spatial clusters.

\subsection{Discussion}
The results support a cautious interpretation of the proposed framework. Grid-aware demand clustering does not dominate all baselines across all metrics. Instead, it changes the planning balance. In the current Barnet case, the demand-weighted baseline is stronger when the objective is primarily user convenience and demand coverage. The grid-aware method is stronger when the objective gives greater importance to investment and grid-risk exposure.

This behaviour is consistent with the motivation of the framework. Grid awareness should not be expected to reduce service distance automatically. Its purpose is to make grid-side pressure visible earlier in the planning process. The practical value of the method therefore depends on how local authorities or network planners value user convenience relative to connection risk, hosting-capacity pressure, and investment cost.

The current results also highlight two limitations. First, grid risk is represented by an open-data proxy rather than measured feeder-level hosting capacity. Second, the optimisation stage uses a lightweight greedy solver for reproducibility and transparency. A future mixed-integer or multi-objective optimiser may improve the trade-off frontier and reduce the unserved demand observed in the grid-aware setting.
